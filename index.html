WHAT ARE QUANTUM COMPUTERS?

Instead of using the standard 1's and 0's (bits), quantum computers use atoms called "qubits" that exist in a state of superposition, meaning they can be in-between 0 and 1 until measured, when it will randomly choose with probabilities based on its current position. This allows them to perform certain calculations exponentially faster than classical computers. Specifically for cryptography, this means quantum computers can run Shor's Algorithm, which can factor the huge numbers that protect your encrypted data in just a couple minutes

WHAT POST-QUANTUM CRYPTOGRAPHY (PQC) IS AND WHY IT MATTERS

POST-QUANTUM CRYPTOGRAPHY, OR PQC FOR SHORT, ARE NEW ENCRYPTION ALGORITHMS DESIGNED TO RESIST ATTACKS FROM BOTH CLASSICAL COMPUTERS AND QUANTUM COMPUTERS. THESE SOLUTIONS EXIST RIGHT NOW.

THE HISTORY

In 1994, Peter Shor published his paper introducing Shor's algorithm, bringing early attention to the problem of PQC. After a couple decades of quantum computers slowly becoming real and improving in their capabilities, governments began to give real thought to PQC migration. In 2016, NIST published a "Report on Post-Quantum Cryptography", beginning their 8-year process of finding potential PQC algorithms. Research teams around the world submitted 82 algorithms, with others finding vulnerabilities and issues with several, before finally ending with 4 selected algorithms to be implemented.

THE FOUR SELECTED PQC ALGORITHMS:

  • FIPS 203: ML-KEM (MODULE-LATTICE-BASED KEY-ENCAPSULATION MECHANISM)

    This is the method used to share an encryption key between two servers, like your phone getting a key from a website's server to ensure communication between the two is encrypted. It uses a lattice-based method, where there is a massive multi-dimensional grid of dots, and the computer moves one of the dots a little bit. Using the key, you can figure out which dot moved, but it is too difficult to do otherwise, even for a quantum computer.

  • FIPS 204: ML-DSA (MODULE-LATTICE-BASED DIGITAL SIGNATURE ALGORITHM)

    This is a signature algorithm, which essentially verifies that a file comes from the person who claimed to make it, and it has not been tampered with. This is necessary for tasks like software updates. This also uses a lattice-based method, but slightly differently. You can verify that a specific person made the file by seeing that their “key,” which is essentially a specific path through the lattice, matches up exactly with the lattice embedded in the file.

  • FIPS 205: SLH-DSA (STATELESS HASH-BASED DIGITAL SIGNATURE ALGORITHM)

    This is the backup method if someone happens to find a way to break the lattice method. This works by using thousands of hash functions, which are functions that take data, and turn it into an unreadable mess of characters. This is also uncrackable by quantum computers, but it is less desirable, because the keys and signatures are longer and the function is slower.

  • FIPS 206: FN-DSA (FFT (FAST-FOURIER TRANSFORM) OVER NTRU-LATTICE-BASED DIGITAL SIGNATURE ALGORITHM)

    This is the method made for small devices. It also uses lattices, but a specific kind known as a NTRU lattice. By using these and tons of complex math using FFT, it ends up creating much smaller keys and signatures. It is perfect for things like Raspberry Pis or places with poor internet connection, where large keys could create a storage or bandwidth bottleneck. The only downside is that it is much harder to implement and the complexity leads to errors occurring while implementing, so it should be used when necessary.

  • LATER ADDITION: HQC (Hamming Quasi-Cyclic)

    This is another alternative published a year after the competition to add more variation rather than only lattice-based methods. This sends the message with built-in error correction arranged in repeated blocks with added noise. The private key can get rid of the noise. This is useful, but has larger key sizes and larger messages compared to lattice-based methods.

HOW TO MIGRATE YOUR SYSTEMS

PQC Migration Roadmap

The Post-Quantum Cryptography Coalition (PQCC) recommends the following phased approach:

Stage Key Activities
Preparation Assign leadership and identity PQC importance across systems. Inventory existing cryptography and make stakeholders aware so the organization is ready to fund, staff, and prioritize migration.
Baseline Understanding Build a complete inventory of cryptographic systems. Prioritize critical assets for migration, identify stakeholders, and develop messaging to align business and technical owners.
Planning and Execution Define migration plans, budgets, and timelines; select and acquire PQC solutions. Implement chosen solutions, and validate proper implementation through testing.
Monitoring and Evaluation Create workforce training and measurable tracking for migration progress. Continuously monitor systems, update controls and processes, and iterate to ensure long-term security.
